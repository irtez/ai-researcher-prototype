# Skill: Abstract Writer

## Purpose

Use this skill when drafting the Abstract section of a full paper.

---

## Role in an Academic Paper

The Abstract is the paper's single most-read section. It must convey the problem, approach, key results, and significance concisely within the task or venue word limit, if specified. Reviewers, area chairs, and readers decide whether to continue based on the Abstract alone.

---

## Required Content

1. **Problem statement**: what problem does this paper address?
2. **Gap or motivation**: why is this problem important and what is missing in current work?
3. **Proposed approach**: what does this paper do? (one or two sentences)
4. **Key results**: the most important empirical or analytical findings.
5. **Significance**: why the results matter or what they enable.

---

## How to Use the Skeleton

- Extract the central claim, problem, gap, main idea, and top contributions.
- Condense these into the Abstract structure above.
- Use the evidence plan to identify which key results to mention.

---

## Previous Sections as Context

The Abstract is typically the first section drafted. No previous sections are available. Use only the skeleton and provided input materials.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Too vague**: "We propose a novel framework and show good results." Fix: state what the framework does and what the results are.
- **Too long**: reads like an introduction or exceeds the requested limit. Fix: keep only the question, approach and supported findings.
- **Missing results**: describes the method but not the outcomes. Fix: include at least one concrete finding.
- **Overclaiming**: uses "state-of-the-art" or "significantly outperforms" without basis. Fix: use calibrated language or leave a placeholder.
- **Inventing numbers**: reports specific metrics not in the provided evidence. Fix: use a TODO placeholder.

---

## What This Section Should NOT Do

- Should not include citations (rare exceptions exist, but default to no citations in the Abstract).
- Should not introduce acronyms or notation not defined in the same sentence.
- Should not list all contributions in detail — that belongs in the Introduction.

---

## Section-Specific Self-Check

After drafting, verify:
- Is the Abstract concise and within any requested word limit?
- Does it cover problem, gap, approach, results, and significance?
- Are all mentioned results backed by available evidence?
- Does it avoid invented metrics or overclaiming?
- Is the central claim consistent with the skeleton?
